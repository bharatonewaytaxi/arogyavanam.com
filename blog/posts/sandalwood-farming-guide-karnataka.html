Karnataka is the heartland of Indian sandalwood, and more landowners now ask whether they can grow it themselves. The short answer is yes, private growers can grow white sandalwood (Santalum album), but it is a long-cycle crop that rewards patience, careful planting and good protection. This guide covers the basics in plain language, so you can decide whether to farm it yourself or choose a managed option.

Why white sandalwood?

White sandalwood is grown for its fragrant heartwood. The scented heartwood forms slowly inside the trunk, so the crop is measured in many years, not seasons. Its fragrance and long association with South India are why people are drawn to it. Because the value sits in the heartwood, everything about sandalwood farming is about keeping the tree healthy and safe for a long time.

It also suits the region. Sandalwood generally does well in dry, warm climates such as the Deccan plateau of south Karnataka.

Host plants: why sandalwood is never grown alone

Sandalwood is semi-parasitic. It makes its own food but also draws water and nutrients from the roots of nearby plants. Farmers therefore plant a host species beside each sapling.

  • Casuarina is a common companion tree.
  • Pulses and fruit trees can also serve as hosts in the early years.
  • Hosts need pruning and management as the sandalwood grows.

Soil and water

Sandalwood generally prefers well-drained red or sandy loam soil and does poorly in waterlogged ground. Standing water is a bigger risk than dry spells once the tree is established.

Young plants need regular watering. Drip irrigation helps save water and keeps moisture even, which is why it is the usual choice on serious plantations. Arogya Vana uses drip irrigation, water management and rainwater harvesting across its plantation.

Care and protection

Good care in the early years sets the tree up for the decades that follow. A typical routine looks like this:

  1. Raise or buy healthy nursery saplings.
  2. Plant each sapling with a host plant beside it.
  3. Water, weed and mulch through the early years.
  4. Prune and manage host plants as the trees grow.
  5. Watch for pests and disease.

Protection matters just as much. Because mature sandalwood is valuable, theft is a real risk on any sandalwood farm. Fencing, watchmen and cameras are part of serious sandalwood farming. At Arogya Vana the land has 24/7 security, CCTV and solar fencing.

A long cycle: think in years

Sandalwood is not a quick crop. The heartwood takes many years to develop, so anyone planting it should be comfortable holding land for the long term. Returns depend on tree growth, protection and market price, and they are not guaranteed. This is also why many families look for income along the way: seasonal fruits and vegetables grown between the trees can provide regular produce while the sandalwood matures.

Legal harvest and sale

In India, sandalwood felling and sale are regulated. Growers generally need to register trees and follow the state forest department's process when harvesting and selling. Rules change, so always check the current position with the Karnataka Forest Department or take legal advice before you plant or sell.
